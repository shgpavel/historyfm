\subtitle{How Greek Geometry Learned to Measure Curved Figures Without Calculus}

\begin{frame}{The Central Question}
        \Large
        How can we measure a curved figure using only straight lines?

        \vspace{0.6cm}
        \normalsize
        Ancient Greek mathematicians could handle triangles, polygons and similar figures. But curves created a conceptual barrier:
        \begin{itemize}
                \item What is the exact area of a circle?
                \item What is the exact area under a parabola?
                \item What is the exact volume of a sphere?
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \textbf{The breakthrough:} reduce the curved problem to a controlled sequence of polygonal approximations.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Barrier: Curves Were Not Polygons}
        Greek geometry was powerful, but it was built around finite constructions.

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \begin{columns}
                \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
                        \textbf{Easy to control}
                        \begin{itemize}
                                \item triangles
                                \item rectangles
                                \item polygons
                                \item ratios of similar figures
                        \end{itemize}
                \end{column}
                \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
                        \textbf{Hard to control}
                        \begin{itemize}
                                \item circles
                                \item spheres
                                \item cones
                                \item parabolic segments
                        \end{itemize}
                \end{column}
        \end{columns}

        \vspace{0.5cm}
        A numerical approximation was not enough. Greek mathematics demanded a proof.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Greek Meta-Mathematics}
        The method of exhaustion was possible because of a specific idea of mathematical knowledge.

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \begin{itemize}
                \item Mathematics was primarily \textbf{geometry of magnitudes}, not algebraic symbolism.
                \item A result had to be established by \textbf{deductive proof}.
                \item Curved objects had to be compared with simpler objects whose areas or volumes were already known.
                \item Infinite processes were avoided as objects, but could be controlled through finite contradiction arguments.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \textbf{Key idea:} do not ``sum infinitely many pieces'' directly. Prove that any remaining error can be made smaller than any assigned magnitude.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Eudoxus' Move: Exhaust the Difference}
        The method is traditionally associated with Eudoxus and later appears systematically in Euclid and Archimedes.

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \textbf{Basic scheme:}
        \begin{enumerate}
                \item Put a polygonal figure inside the curved figure.
                \item Improve the polygon so that it fills more of the curved figure.
                \item Show that the leftover area or volume can be made arbitrarily small.
                \item Use contradiction to prove the exact result.
        \end{enumerate}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        The figure is not literally destroyed. The \textbf{possible error} is exhausted.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Euclid: Turning the Method into Rigour}
        Euclid's \emph{Elements}, Book XII, uses exhaustion to prove results about circles, cones, cylinders and spheres.

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        Example result:
        \[
                \frac{\text{area of circle } C_1}{\text{area of circle } C_2}
                =
                \frac{d_1^2}{d_2^2}.
        \]

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        In modern language, this says that circle areas scale like the square of their diameters.

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \textbf{Important:} this is not just measurement. It is a proof that all circles obey the same area law.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Archimedes and the Circle}
        Archimedes used polygonal bounds to trap the circumference of a circle.

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \begin{itemize}
                \item An inscribed polygon gives a lower bound.
                \item A circumscribed polygon gives an upper bound.
                \item Increasing the number of sides tightens the bounds.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        His famous estimate:
        \[
                3 + \frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3 + \frac{1}{7}.
        \]

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \textbf{Suggested visual:} circle with an inscribed and circumscribed regular polygon; then show the same circle with more sides.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Archimedes and the Parabola}
        In \emph{Quadrature of the Parabola}, Archimedes proved that a parabolic segment has area
        \[
                \text{area of segment} = \frac{4}{3}\cdot \text{area of the main triangle}.
        \]

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        The strategy:
        \begin{itemize}
                \item inscribe a triangle in the parabolic segment;
                \item add smaller triangles in the remaining gaps;
                \item show that the total uncovered area becomes negligible;
                \item conclude the exact area by exhaustion.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        This is close in spirit to summing a geometric series, but it is expressed as Greek geometry.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why This Was a Breakthrough}
        The method of exhaustion crossed a major boundary:

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \begin{block}{Before}
                Exact geometry worked best for straight-edged figures.
        \end{block}

        \begin{block}{After}
                Curved figures could be treated with the same deductive seriousness.
        \end{block}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \textbf{The deeper innovation:} Greek mathematicians created a rigorous way to reason about limiting processes without having a modern concept of limit.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Not Calculus Yet}
        It is tempting to call exhaustion ``ancient integral calculus'', but this is only partly correct.

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \begin{columns}
                \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
                        \textbf{Similar to calculus}
                        \begin{itemize}
                                \item approximation by simpler pieces
                                \item error control
                                \item exact areas and volumes
                        \end{itemize}
                \end{column}
                \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
                        \textbf{Different from calculus}
                        \begin{itemize}
                                \item no symbolic limit notation
                                \item no general integral operator
                                \item proof is case-by-case and geometric
                        \end{itemize}
                \end{column}
        \end{columns}

        \vspace{0.5cm}
        Exhaustion was not a calculus algorithm. It was a standard of proof for difficult geometric results.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Role of Meta-Mathematics}
        The breakthrough depended on how Greek mathematicians understood mathematics itself.

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \begin{itemize}
                \item They separated \textbf{exact proof} from practical measurement.
                \item They treated areas and volumes as \textbf{magnitudes} comparable by ratios.
                \item They accepted indirect proof as a legitimate tool.
                \item They controlled the infinite through finite logical arguments.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        So the method of exhaustion was not merely a technique. It was a new way of making curved geometry rigorous.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Afterlife of the Idea}
        The method of exhaustion did not disappear.

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        \begin{itemize}
                \item It influenced later approaches to area and volume.
                \item It anticipated central ideas of integral calculus.
                \item It showed that approximation and exact proof can work together.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \textbf{Historical bridge:}
        \[
                \text{Greek exhaustion} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{early modern indivisibles} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \text{integral calculus}
        \]

        \vspace{0.3cm}
        The ancient Greeks did not have calculus, but they created one of its deepest ancestors.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
        \Large
        The method of exhaustion solved a problem that was both technical and philosophical.

        \vspace{0.5cm}
        \normalsize
        \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{Barrier:} curved figures resisted finite geometric measurement.
                \item \textbf{Breakthrough:} polygonal approximations with rigorous error control.
                \item \textbf{Meta-mathematics:} proof, ratios, contradiction and exactness mattered more than numerical approximation.
        \end{itemize}

        \vspace{0.4cm}
        \textbf{Final thesis:} Greek mathematics overcame curves by turning approximation into proof.
\end{frame}
